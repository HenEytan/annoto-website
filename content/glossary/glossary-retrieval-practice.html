Most study habits are about putting information in: re-reading notes, highlighting, re-watching a lecture. Retrieval practice reverses the direction. Instead of reviewing material, the learner tries to pull it back out of memory, by answering a question, explaining a concept without looking, or solving a problem from scratch. The act of recalling is not just a measurement of what was learned. It is one of the most effective ways to make learning stick, and it is the principle that makes in-video questions work.

Why recalling beats reviewing

Every successful retrieval strengthens the memory trace and adds new routes back to it, which makes the information easier to find next time. Reviewing feels productive because the material looks familiar, but familiarity is not the same as being able to use it. Learners who re-watch a video often overestimate what they know; learners who have to answer a question discover their gaps while there is still time to fix them. That feedback loop is why retrieval practice improves both memory and the accuracy of learners' judgments about their own understanding.

What the research says

The testing effect is one of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology. In a frequently cited 2006 study, Roediger and Karpicke found that students who practised recalling a passage remembered substantially more a week later than students who spent the same time re-studying it, even though the re-studiers felt more confident. Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 review of learning techniques rated practice testing as one of only two techniques with high utility across ages, subjects and materials. The effect holds for low-stakes and ungraded questions, which matters for course design: the benefit comes from the act of retrieving, not from the grade attached to it.

Retrieval practice inside course video

Video is where retrieval practice is most often missing. A recorded lecture asks nothing of the viewer, so an hour of watching can produce an hour of familiarity and very little recall. Interpolated questions fix that. A question placed just after a concept forces the viewer to retrieve it while it is still fresh, and the expectation of the next question changes how the rest of the video is watched. Reflection prompts extend the same idea to open-ended recall: asking learners to explain a step in their own words is retrieval, and in Annoto the explanation becomes a timestamped comment that peers and instructors can respond to. Making it stick: spacing and feedback

Two design choices multiply the effect. First, space it out: retrieving the same idea again after a gap of days is more durable than retrieving it three times in one sitting, so pair in-video questions with a follow-up quiz a week later (see spaced practice). Second, give feedback. A wrong answer with no explanation can cement the error; a one-sentence explanation attached to each option corrects it at the moment the learner is paying most attention. Quiz analytics then show which items most learners miss, so the explanation before them can be improved.

Frequently asked questions

Is retrieval practice the same as testing?

It overlaps with testing but the goal is different. A test measures what a learner knows; retrieval practice uses the act of recalling to strengthen what they know. Low-stakes, frequent, ungraded or lightly graded questions produce the benefit without the anxiety of high-stakes assessment.

How often should learners practise retrieval in a video?

A question every three to six minutes of video is a practical rhythm supported by research on interpolated testing. Add a follow-up retrieval activity a few days later so the practice is spaced as well as frequent.

Does retrieval practice work for complex or applied subjects?

Yes. Reviews of the research find the benefit across subjects and for applied as well as factual material. For complex skills, use application or explanation prompts rather than recognition questions, so learners retrieve the reasoning and not just the terms.
