Working memory, the mental workspace where new information is processed, can only hold a few elements at a time. Cognitive load theory, developed by John Sweller from the late 1980s, starts from that limit. If a lesson asks learners to juggle too much at once, such as reading dense slides while listening to a different explanation, the capacity left for actually understanding the material shrinks. Design choices that seem cosmetic can decide whether a video teaches.

Three types of load

Intrinsic load comes from the complexity of the material itself; it can be managed by sequencing but not removed. Extraneous load comes from poor design, such as clutter, redundant text or unclear visuals, and should be cut. Germane load is the productive effort of building understanding, such as connecting a new idea to what the learner already knows. The goal is to minimise extraneous load so capacity is available for germane processing.

Mayer's principles for multimedia

Richard Mayer's research on multimedia learning turned the theory into practical rules for slides and video. Among the best supported: coherence (remove interesting but irrelevant material), signalling (highlight what matters), redundancy (do not put the full narration on screen as text), segmenting (break a lesson into learner-paced parts) and pre-training (teach key terms before the main explanation).

Applying the principles to lecture video

CoherenceDecorative animations and tangentsKeep only visuals that carry the explanation

Interaction without overload

Adding interaction to video can reduce load or add to it. A question at a natural pause segments the lesson and gives working memory time to consolidate. A pop-up that interrupts mid-sentence adds extraneous load. Place questions and prompts at concept boundaries, keep them focused on the idea just taught, and let learners take notes against the timeline so they are not holding everything in their heads.

Frequently asked questions

What are the three types of cognitive load?

Intrinsic load from the material's complexity, extraneous load from poor design, and germane load from the effort of building understanding. Good instruction reduces extraneous load and manages intrinsic load.

Do in-video questions increase cognitive load?

Not when they are well placed. Questions at concept boundaries act as segmenting and help consolidation. Questions that interrupt an explanation midway or test unrelated details add unnecessary load.

Should lecture slides contain full sentences?

Generally no, if the same words are being spoken. The redundancy principle suggests short keywords or visuals on screen with the explanation in narration, while providing a full transcript separately for accessibility.
