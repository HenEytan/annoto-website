Every cohort contains learners who read slowly or quickly, who are watching on a phone on a commute, who are working in a second language, who have a hearing or attention difference, or who simply engage better by talking than by writing. Universal Design for Learning (UDL) treats that variability as the normal case. Instead of designing for an imagined average learner and adding accommodations afterwards, UDL builds flexibility into the course from the first draft. The framework is maintained by CAST, which published an updated version of the guidelines in 2024.

The three principles

UDL is organised around three principles. Multiple means of engagement: offer different ways to spark interest and sustain effort, such as choice, relevance and community. Multiple means of representation: present information in more than one format, such as video with captions, a transcript and a written summary. Multiple means of action and expression: let learners show understanding in more than one way, such as a written answer, a spoken reply or a short video.

What UDL means for course video

A lecture video on its own offers one means of representation and no means of expression. That is the gap UDL asks designers to close. Accurate captions and a transcript cover representation for learners who cannot or prefer not to listen. Personal notes and AI summaries give learners a second, text-based route back into the material. Timestamped discussion adds engagement through community, and letting learners respond with text, audio or video covers action and expression.

UDL and legal accessibility

UDL and accessibility law overlap but are not the same. Accessibility requirements, such as the WCAG 2.1 AA standard referenced by the updated ADA Title II rule for US public institutions, set a minimum: content and tools must be usable with assistive technology. UDL goes further, aiming for courses that work well for everyone rather than merely being technically accessible. Meeting the legal floor is necessary; UDL is how courses become genuinely usable. Frequently asked questions

Is UDL only for students with disabilities?

No. UDL is designed for all learners. Features such as captions, transcripts and multiple response formats help learners with disabilities and also help second-language learners, commuters watching without sound and anyone reviewing for an exam.

What are the three principles of UDL?

Multiple means of engagement, multiple means of representation, and multiple means of action and expression. Together they ask designers to vary why, what and how learners learn.

How do I start applying UDL to existing course videos?

Start with captions and transcripts, add a short text summary, and give learners at least one way to respond to the video other than a written essay, such as a timestamped comment or a short spoken reply.
