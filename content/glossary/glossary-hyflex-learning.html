The HyFlex model was developed by Brian Beatty at San Francisco State University in the mid-2000s to serve students who could not always come to campus. It became widely known during and after the pandemic, when many institutions needed courses that worked for students in the room, students joining by video call and students who could only watch later. In a HyFlex course the student, not the instructor, chooses the mode for each session, and all three modes are expected to lead to the same outcomes.

The four HyFlex values

Beatty's model rests on four values. Learner choice: meaningful alternatives, so students can pick a mode week by week. Equivalency: activities in every mode lead to equivalent learning. Reusability: artifacts from one mode, such as recordings and discussion, are used as learning objects in the others. Accessibility: students have the technology and skills to use every mode.

Where HyFlex breaks down

In practice the asynchronous path is usually the weakest. In-room students get discussion and questions; online students can at least speak up; asynchronous students often get a recording and nothing else. They watch alone, cannot ask questions at the moment they are confused, and leave no evidence of participation. That undermines equivalency, and for online programs in the US it can also create problems with regular and substantive interaction requirements.

Reusing the room's contributions

Reusability is the HyFlex value most often ignored. When the recording of a session carries the questions asked in the room and the discussion that followed, asynchronous students inherit that conversation instead of watching a lecture in isolation. With a video engagement layer such as Annoto, comments added by asynchronous students are anchored to the same moments, so the recording becomes a shared space that grows across sections and semesters.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between hybrid and HyFlex?

In a hybrid or blended course, the instructor decides which parts happen in person and which online. In a HyFlex course, students choose their mode for each session, and every session must work in all modes.

Is HyFlex harder to teach?

Usually yes, at least at first, because the instructor is serving three audiences at once. Designing activities that work the same way in every mode, such as questions and discussion anchored to the recording, reduces the load over time.

How do I track participation of asynchronous HyFlex students?

Use activities that leave a record: in-video questions, timestamped comments and completion tracking. Syncing that data to the LMS gradebook gives asynchronous students a participation record comparable to attendance.
