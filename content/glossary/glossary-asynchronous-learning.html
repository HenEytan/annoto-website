In a synchronous course, learning happens together at a set time, in a room or on a video call. In an asynchronous course, it happens across a window of time: a learner might watch this week's lecture on Tuesday night, post in the discussion on Thursday and respond to a peer on Saturday. Most online courses, and a growing share of on-campus courses, are largely asynchronous because it lets working adults, students in different time zones and anyone with caregiving responsibilities participate.

Benefits

Flexibility is the obvious one. Learners also get time to think before they respond, which can produce more considered contributions than a live discussion, and quieter students often participate more. Recorded material can be paused, replayed and reviewed before exams. For institutions, asynchronous courses scale to more learners and more locations.

Pitfalls

The main risk is isolation. Without the cues of a shared session, learners can drift, and instructors lose sight of who is struggling until assignments are missed. Discussion boards detached from the content often become compliance exercises: one post, two replies, no real conversation. For online programs in the US there is also a regulatory dimension, since distance education must provide regular and substantive interaction between instructors and students to be distinguished from correspondence study.

Asynchronous does not mean alone

The strongest asynchronous courses feel social. When learners can see each other's questions and comments at the moment in the video they relate to, and when the instructor responds there, the recording becomes a shared space rather than a broadcast. Faculty at institutions such as Florida State University have used this approach to create an in-person feeling in fully asynchronous courses.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between synchronous and asynchronous learning?

Synchronous learning happens at the same time for everyone, such as a live class or webinar. Asynchronous learning happens on each learner's own schedule within deadlines, such as recorded lectures, discussion threads and self-paced activities.

How do you keep students engaged in asynchronous courses?

Give them something to do in every video, make discussion specific to moments in the content, keep instructor presence visible, and use analytics to reach out to learners who fall behind early.

Does asynchronous learning meet RSI requirements?

It can, if the course includes regular, instructor-initiated, substantive interaction such as feedback, answering questions and facilitating discussion, and if that interaction is documented.
