Cramming works, briefly. Studying everything in one long session produces good performance the next morning and steep forgetting after that. Spaced practice, also called distributed practice, spreads the same amount of study across several sessions separated by time. The total effort is the same; the retention is not. Material revisited after a gap is remembered far longer than material studied in a single block, which is why spacing is one of the few learning techniques that researchers recommend almost without qualification.

Why gaps help

When material is revisited immediately, it is still in working memory, so the second pass takes little effort and adds little. After a gap, some forgetting has happened and the learner has to reconstruct the idea. That reconstruction is harder, and the difficulty is productive: it strengthens the memory and links it to more cues. Psychologists call this a desirable difficulty. It also explains why spaced study often feels less effective to learners than cramming, even when it produces better results.

What the research says

The spacing effect has been documented since Ebbinghaus's memory experiments in the 1880s. A 2006 meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues, covering hundreds of experiments, confirmed that spaced study reliably beats massed study and that the best gap grows with how long the learner needs to remember: for a test in a week, a gap of a day or so works well; for retention across a semester or a career, gaps of weeks are better. Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 review rated distributed practice, alongside practice testing, as a high-utility technique.

Spacing in a video-based course

Most course calendars are built around new content every week, so earlier material is rarely revisited until the final exam. Video makes spacing cheap to add. A short review clip from week two can be reused in week five with two retrieval questions attached. A recap question at the start of each new lecture can ask about the previous one. Activity completion and gradebook sync make these return visits count, so learners treat them as part of the course rather than optional extras.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is spacing re-reading instead of spacing retrieval. Re-watching a video a week later helps less than answering questions about it a week later. The second is making every review high-stakes, which pushes learners back to cramming. Keep spaced activities short, low-stakes and frequent, and let the analytics show which topics need another return visit.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between spaced practice and retrieval practice?

Retrieval practice is about how learners study (recalling rather than reviewing); spaced practice is about when they study (spread out rather than massed). They combine well: the strongest approach is spaced retrieval, meaning short question sets on the same material repeated over days and weeks.

How long should the gap be?

It depends on how long the knowledge must last. As a rule of thumb, the gap should be roughly 10 to 20 percent of the retention interval: about a day for a test in a week, a week or more for material that must last a semester.

Can spacing be automated in an LMS?

Partly. Instructors can schedule review videos and quizzes with release dates, and tools like Annoto can attach questions to reused clips and sync completion to the gradebook. Deciding which topics to revisit is still best guided by quiz analytics showing where learners struggled.
