When a university buys software, its accessibility office needs to know whether students and staff who use screen readers, keyboard navigation or captions can actually use it. The VPAT is how vendors answer that question in a consistent format. The template is maintained by the Information Technology Industry Council (ITI) and comes in editions for WCAG, US Section 508, the European standard EN 301 549, and an international edition covering all three. A completed VPAT is formally called an Accessibility Conformance Report, though most people still say VPAT.

What a VPAT contains

The core of a VPAT is a table listing each accessibility criterion, such as each WCAG 2.1 success criterion at levels A and AA, with a conformance level and remarks. The standard conformance levels are Supports, Partially Supports, Does Not Support and Not Applicable. The remarks column is where the useful detail lives: which features are affected, what workarounds exist and what is planned.

Why VPATs matter more now

In the US, the Department of Justice's 2024 rule under ADA Title II requires state and local government entities, including public colleges and universities, to make web content and mobile apps conform to WCAG 2.1 AA, with compliance deadlines that depend on the size of the entity. That includes third-party tools used in courses. Procurement and accessibility teams are asking for current VPATs earlier in the buying process, and a missing or outdated one can stall a purchase. Frequently asked questions

Is a VPAT legally required?

The VPAT itself is voluntary, as the name says. But many public institutions require one in procurement because they are legally responsible for the accessibility of the tools they use.

What is the difference between a VPAT and an ACR?

The VPAT is the blank template; an Accessibility Conformance Report (ACR) is the completed document for a specific product. In practice the two terms are used interchangeably.

Does a VPAT prove a product is accessible?

No. It is the vendor's report of conformance. Treat it as a starting point, check the remarks, and test key workflows with assistive technology before rollout.
