Student video is everywhere: presentations, pitches, language speaking tasks, clinical skills, teaching demonstrations, lab procedures. Feedback on it has not always kept up. The common pattern is to watch the whole video, then write a paragraph at the end: "Your introduction was strong, but the middle section dragged and some terms were used loosely." The student then has to rewatch and guess which moments you meant.

What changes with timestamps

Timestamped feedback means commenting at the exact moment in the video the comment refers to. The student clicks the comment and the video jumps to that point. It sounds like a small change. In practice it changes three things:

  • Specificity. "At 2:14 you define the term correctly, but at 3:40 you use it for something different" is actionable. "Some terms were used loosely" is not.
  • Speed. You comment while watching instead of taking notes and rewriting them at the end. Most instructors find they spend less time per video.
  • Tone. Short comments on specific moments read as coaching rather than a verdict, and it is natural to mix praise and suggestions throughout.

A workflow for one submission

  1. Watch at normal speed once, commenting as you go. Aim for three to six comments on a five-minute video.
  2. Tag comments to rubric criteria where your tool allows it, so each comment is evidence for a score.
  3. Mark the single most important improvement. Make it clear which comment matters most.
  4. Score the rubric, using your timestamped comments as evidence.
  5. Add one summary sentence at the end pointing to the key comment.

Beyond the instructor

The same approach works for peer and self-assessment. Students reviewing a classmate's video with a rubric and timestamped comments give far more useful feedback than end-of-video notes; see our free peer review rubric for video presentations. Asking students to add three timestamped self-reflections to their own video before submitting, such as "I would redo this part because...", builds reflective practice and often answers your feedback before you give it.

Where it is used

Timestamped feedback is particularly valuable in communication courses, language learning, nursing and clinical skills and teacher education, where the performance itself is what is being assessed. Faculty at Washington University in St. Louis have used it for time-tagged feedback on student sign-language videos.
