"Post one comment and reply to two classmates" produces exactly what it asks for: one comment and two replies, most of them generic. The fix is usually not a new tool or a stricter rubric. It is a better prompt, placed at the right moment. Below are 30 prompts you can copy into your next course video, grouped by what you want students to do.

Why placement matters as much as wording

A prompt at the end of a 40-minute video asks students to remember and summarise everything. A prompt pinned to the exact moment a concept is explained asks them to react to something specific while it is on screen. The second produces more concrete, more varied answers, and makes it natural for classmates to respond to each other. Wherever possible, anchor each prompt to a timestamp.

Checking understanding

  1. In one sentence, what is the main claim made in this section?
  2. Which step in this explanation would you find hardest to explain to a friend? Why?
  3. What is one question you would ask the speaker right now?
  4. Rewrite the definition just given in your own words, without using any of the same key terms.
  5. What is the most common mistake someone might make applying this? How would you avoid it?
  6. What was the muddiest point in this segment for you?

Connecting to experience

  1. Where have you seen this concept in your own work, studies or daily life?
  2. Describe a situation where this approach would not work. What would you do instead?
  3. How would someone in a different role or field see this differently?
  4. What surprised you here, compared with what you expected before watching?
  5. If you had to teach this to a first-year student tomorrow, what example would you use?
  6. Which earlier topic in the course does this connect to, and how?

Applying and predicting

  1. Pause here. What do you predict happens next, and why?
  2. Apply this method to the following case: [short scenario]. What result do you get?
  3. What would change if [one variable] were doubled?
  4. Propose a second example that fits this principle, different from the one shown.
  5. Which of the options just discussed would you choose for [scenario], and what is your reasoning?
  6. What information would you need before making this decision in practice?

Evaluating and arguing

  1. Do you agree with the argument made at this point? Give one reason for and one against.
  2. What evidence would change your mind about this claim?
  3. What is the strongest objection someone could raise here?
  4. Is the data shown enough to support the conclusion? What is missing?
  5. Rank the three approaches presented from most to least useful for [context], and justify your top choice.
  6. Whose perspective is missing from this example?

Peer interaction

  1. Find a classmate's comment you disagree with, and explain your view respectfully with one piece of evidence.
  2. Build on a classmate's example by adding a detail or a complication.
  3. Answer a question another student asked at this timestamp.
  4. Compare your prediction with two classmates'. Whose reasoning was most convincing?
  5. Summarise the discussion so far at this point in three bullet points.
  6. Suggest one follow-up question for the whole class based on the comments here.

Tips for better answers

  • Use fewer prompts, better placed. Two or three anchored prompts per video beat ten generic ones.
  • Model the answer. Post the first reply yourself, or pin a strong example from a previous cohort.
  • Allow different formats. Let students respond with a short audio or video reply as well as text. It suits different learners and produces more natural answers, especially in language courses.
  • Show up in the thread. A few instructor replies in the first days set the tone and count as instructor-initiated interaction for RSI.
  • Close the loop. Mention a strong comment in the next video or announcement. Students notice when their contributions are read.
