Ask any instructional designer how long a lecture video should be and you will probably hear "six minutes". The number is real, it comes from good data, and it is also routinely misapplied. Whether your recordings are five minutes or fifty, the more useful question is not how long they are but what learners are asked to do while watching.

Where the six-minute figure comes from

The figure traces back to a 2014 study by Philip Guo, Juho Kim and Rob Rubin, who analysed millions of video-watching sessions on edX. They found that median engagement time, meaning how long learners kept watching, was roughly six minutes regardless of how long the video was. Learners watched most of a short video, but only a fraction of a long one. Shorter videos also scored better on their measure of whether learners attempted the follow-up problems.

That is a strong signal, but note what it measured: behaviour in open online courses, where learners had no grade riding on the video and could leave at any time. In a for-credit course, students will often sit through a long recording because they have to. That does not mean they are learning from all of it.

Attention is not a fixed clock

The popular claim that "attention spans are ten minutes" is weaker than it sounds. Reviews of the lecture-attention literature have found little solid evidence for a fixed attention limit. What the evidence does support is that attention drifts when nothing is asked of the learner, and that it recovers when the task changes. Research on interpolated testing, notably Szpunar, Khan and Schacter's 2013 study, found that inserting short questions throughout recorded lectures reduced mind-wandering and improved final test scores, without making the lecture any shorter.

In other words, the problem with a long video is not its length. It is a long stretch with nothing to do.

Practical guidance by video type

Video typeTarget lengthWhat to add
Concept explainer3 to 6 minutesOne retrieval question just after the key point
Worked example or demonstration5 to 12 minutesA prediction question before the key step, a check after
Recorded lecture segment10 to 15 minutesA question at each concept boundary, roughly every 4 to 6 minutes
Full recorded class session45 to 75 minutesChapter markers, 8 to 12 questions, a discussion prompt, a closing synthesis question

If you can re-record: segment

If you are producing new content, cut at concept boundaries, not at the clock. One idea per video, as short as that idea allows. Richard Mayer's research on multimedia learning calls this the segmenting principle: learners do better when a complex lesson is presented in learner-paced parts rather than one continuous stream. Give each segment a clear title so learners can find it again when revising.

If you cannot re-record: add structure

Most instructors are not going to re-cut years of lecture capture. The good news is that you do not need to. You can make an existing long recording behave like a series of segments by adding structure on top of it:

  • Questions at concept boundaries. A short in-video question every four to six minutes turns one long video into a sequence of watch-and-check cycles.
  • A discussion prompt at the hardest moment. Pin a question at the point where students usually get confused and let them discuss it there, anchored to the timestamp.
  • Notes and summaries. Let learners take timestamped notes, and give them an AI-generated summary to review, so they are not relying on memory of a one-hour stream.
  • Completion that counts. Sync completion and question scores to the gradebook so watching is part of the course, not optional homework.

Use your own data

General guidance only gets you so far. Once your videos have questions and engagement tracking, your own data will tell you where attention drops, which segments are re-watched, and which questions most students miss. A segment that many students re-watch and still answer incorrectly needs a better explanation, not a shorter one. Our guide to video engagement analytics covers what to look at.

The short answer

Keep new concept videos short, around six minutes or less. For anything longer, stop worrying about the total length and make sure learners have something to do every few minutes. A 50-minute recording with ten well-placed questions will usually teach more than five unconnected ten-minute videos with none.
