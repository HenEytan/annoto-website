A typical course calendar introduces a new topic every week and returns to old ones only at the midterm and final. That is close to the opposite of what memory research recommends. Two of the best-supported learning techniques, retrieval practice and spaced practice, work best together: short, repeated attempts to recall material, spread out over time. The good news is that you can build this into a course using videos you already have.

The two principles in one paragraph

Retrieval practice means learning by recalling, for example by answering questions, rather than by re-reading or re-watching. Spaced practice means returning to material after a gap instead of all at once. Studies going back decades, summarised in Dunlosky and colleagues' 2013 review of learning techniques, rate both as high-utility techniques across subjects and ages. Combined as spaced retrieval, they produce learning that lasts well beyond the exam.

A weekly pattern

You do not need a complex algorithm. A simple, repeating pattern covers most of the benefit:

WhereWhatCovers
This week's lecture video3 to 8 in-video questions at concept boundariesThis week's content, immediate retrieval
Start of the next lecture video2 recap questions in the first minutesLast week's content, first spaced retrieval
A short review clip every two to three weeksA 3 to 5 minute clip from an earlier lecture with 2 application questionsContent from 2 to 4 weeks ago, longer-gap retrieval
Before each examA mixed practice set across all topics so farEverything, interleaved

Choosing the questions

  • Immediate questions check the concept just explained. Multiple choice with feedback on each option works well.
  • Recap questions should require recall, not recognition where possible. "Which of these is the definition of..." is easier than "What happens when...".
  • Review-clip questions should ask learners to apply an older idea to a new case. That strengthens transfer as well as memory.
  • Mixed sets should interleave topics, so learners have to decide which idea applies. That is closer to what exams and real problems demand.

Keep the stakes low

Spaced retrieval works because it is frequent. If every recap question carries significant marks, students cram for them, which defeats the purpose. Grade for completion, or give accuracy a small weight, and let the benefit show up on the exams. Syncing completion to the gradebook keeps participation high without making each question high-stakes.

Let the data decide what to revisit

You cannot revisit everything. Question analytics tell you where to spend review time. If a concept from week three is still answered poorly on the week-five recap, it gets a review clip in week six. If the cohort has clearly mastered something, drop it from the next mixed set. See attention and comprehension analytics for how to spot these patterns per question and per learner.

Tell students why

Spaced retrieval feels harder than re-watching and can feel less productive, even when it works better. A short explanation at the start of the course, such as "these questions are how you will remember this in May", reduces pushback. Showing students their own improvement on recap questions over time is even more persuasive.
