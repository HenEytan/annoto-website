Generative AI has changed what a take-home assignment proves. A polished essay, a discussion post or a short-answer response can now be produced in seconds, and AI detectors are not reliable enough to settle the question. Many instructors have responded by moving assessment back into the classroom. That is not always possible for online, asynchronous or large courses. There is another option: assignments where the evidence of learning is the learner's own reasoning, tied to a specific moment, in their own voice.

Why detection is the wrong battle

Detection tools produce false positives, can be defeated by paraphrasing, and put instructors in the position of accusing students on probabilistic evidence. Many institutions have advised caution in using them for academic integrity decisions. A more durable strategy is to design assignments where outsourcing is harder than doing the work, and where the process of learning is visible, not just the final product.

What makes an assignment harder to outsource

  • It is anchored to specific content. A response to "the argument at 14:32 of this week's lecture" requires having watched it. Generic prompts get generic, easily generated answers.
  • It is spoken or shown, not just written. Explaining a concept aloud, demonstrating a skill, or talking through a worked problem reveals understanding in a way that is harder to script convincingly.
  • It happens in steps. A sequence of small, timestamped contributions across a course is much harder to fake than one final submission.
  • It is social. Responding to a specific classmate's comment, in context, requires engaging with the actual discussion.
  • It asks for the learner's own context. Reflection on a personal experience, a local case or the learner's own previous work is hard to generate generically.

Five video-based assignment designs

1. Timestamped reflection

Pin a reflection prompt to a key moment in a lecture: "Pause here. Explain in your own words why the curve bends at this point." Responses are anchored to that moment and can be text, audio or a short video.

2. Explain-it-back video

Students record a two-minute video explaining one concept to a peer, as if teaching it. It is quick to review, shows depth of understanding, and doubles as retrieval practice for the student.

3. Annotated problem walk-through

Students record themselves solving a problem and narrating their reasoning. The instructor or peers comment at the step where the reasoning goes wrong. The process, not just the answer, becomes the evidence.

4. Response to a peer, in context

Instead of "reply to two classmates", ask students to respond to a specific classmate's comment at a specific timestamp, adding a counter-example or complication.

5. Skills demonstration with rubric feedback

In communication, languages, nursing, teacher education and many professional programs, a recorded performance with rubric-based, timestamped feedback is both authentic assessment and very difficult to outsource.

Keeping it fair and manageable

  • Keep videos short. Two to three minutes is enough to see understanding and keeps review time reasonable.
  • Offer alternatives. Some students are uncomfortable on camera. Audio-only or screen-recorded responses can serve the same purpose, and UDL principles favour offering choice.
  • Use peers for formative rounds. Rubric-guided peer review spreads the feedback load and helps reviewers learn.
  • Be transparent about AI use. Say which uses of AI are allowed, such as brainstorming or checking grammar, and which are not.
