Video presentations are now a standard assignment across disciplines, from business pitches to language practice to clinical demonstrations. Grading them all in detail is slow, and generic peer feedback ("really good, nice slides") does not help anyone improve. A focused rubric fixes most of the problem. Below is a template you can copy, adapt and use for peer review in your next course.

The rubric

Four criteria, three levels each. Keep it short: reviewers give better feedback on four clear criteria than on ten vague ones.

CriterionDeveloping (1)Proficient (2)Excellent (3)
StructurePurpose unclear; ideas appear in no obvious orderClear purpose and order, with some abrupt transitionsClear opening, logical flow, strong conclusion that returns to the purpose
Content accuracyKey concepts missing or used incorrectlyConcepts mostly correct with minor errorsConcepts accurate, precise and applied to the topic
Evidence and examplesClaims made without supportSome claims supported with examples or dataMain claims supported with relevant, well-chosen evidence
DeliveryHard to follow; pace, audio or visuals distractGenerally clear with some distractionsClear, confident, well paced; visuals support the message

Feedback prompts

Scores alone do not help the presenter improve. Ask each reviewer to answer three prompts, and to attach each comment to the moment in the video it refers to:

  1. Strongest moment: At what point was the presentation most effective, and why?
  2. One thing to improve: What single, specific change would most improve this presentation? Point to the moment.
  3. A question for the presenter: What would you ask if you were in the audience?

"One thing to improve" is deliberately singular. A single actionable suggestion is more likely to be used than a list of eight.

Train reviewers before the real round

Peer assessment research consistently finds that reliability improves when reviewers practise with the criteria first. A 20-minute calibration exercise makes a large difference:

  • Share one example video from a previous cohort (with permission) or record a short example yourself.
  • Ask everyone to score it with the rubric and write the three feedback comments.
  • Show your own scores and comments, and discuss where the class disagreed and why.

Setting up the round

  • Two to four reviewers per video. Several reviewers average out individual bias and give the presenter a range of views.
  • Consider anonymity. Anonymous reviews reduce social pressure to be nice, particularly in small cohorts.
  • Grade the reviews. Award a small part of the grade for the quality of feedback given. It signals that reviewing is part of the learning, and it is.
  • Allow a revision. Peer feedback is most valuable when there is a chance to use it. Build in a resubmission or a follow-up presentation.

Why timestamps matter

General comments at the end of a video leave the presenter guessing which moment the reviewer meant. Comments anchored to the timeline remove that guesswork: the presenter clicks the comment and sees exactly the slide, sentence or gesture it refers to. Instructors can also scan the timeline and see where several reviewers flagged the same moment.

Annoto's peer review combines rubric criteria with timestamped comments on video submissions, inside your LMS, with results synced to the gradebook. Faculty at Washington University in St. Louis have used it to make peer feedback more specific and more engaging.

Adapting the rubric

Swap the criteria to fit the task. For language courses, replace "Evidence" with pronunciation and fluency. For clinical skills, replace "Structure" with correct sequence of steps and "Delivery" with communication with the patient. For teacher education, use criteria from your observation framework. Keep the three feedback prompts: they work for almost any performance.
