HyFlex courses let students choose, session by session, whether to attend in the room, join live online, or catch up asynchronously. The model's central promise is equivalency: every path should lead to the same learning outcomes. In practice there is usually a clear hierarchy. In-room students get discussion and questions. Live online students can at least unmute. Asynchronous students get a recording, and nothing else.

Why the asynchronous track falls behind

The live session is designed first and the recording is a by-product. Everything interactive, such as the instructor's questions, the pair discussion, the moment a student asks about a confusing slide, happens once and is either lost or appears on the recording as something to watch rather than join. Asynchronous students have no way to answer the question the instructor asked the room, and no way to ask their own at the moment they are confused.

That has consequences. Asynchronous students tend to disengage, and instructors have little evidence of their participation. For online and distance programs in the US, it also matters for regular and substantive interaction requirements.

Design once, run in three modes

The fix is to design each interactive moment so it works in every mode. Brian Beatty's HyFlex model calls this reusability: artifacts from one mode become learning objects in the others.

A workflow that fits a teaching week

  1. Before class: Prepare two or three check questions and one discussion prompt as part of your normal slides.
  2. During class: Use them live with the room and online students.
  3. After class: When the recording is available, add the same questions at the same moments, and pin the discussion prompt. Add one comment summarising what the room concluded.
  4. During the week: Asynchronous students answer and discuss on the recording. Reply to a few comments, and point out good ones in the next session.

AI can shorten step 3. Annoto's Lumo can draft questions from the recording's transcript for you to approve.

Make the recording a shared space

The biggest change is conceptual. Instead of treating the recording as an archive of something that happened, treat it as a space where the conversation continues. When asynchronous students can see each other's comments and the instructor's replies at the exact moment they relate to, the recording stops being a second-class experience. It can even become the best version of the session, carrying the room's discussion plus a week of additional contributions.

Measuring equivalency

Equivalency is hard to claim without data. Compare question accuracy and participation across modes. If asynchronous students consistently answer the same questions less accurately, the recording needs more support, such as a clearer explanation at that point or an instructor note.
