Canvas has useful built-in reporting. Course analytics show page views, participation and assignment submissions, and let instructors see which students are active. For video-heavy courses, though, there is a gap. Knowing that a student opened the page with this week's lecture does not tell you whether they watched it, which parts they watched, or whether they understood it.

What standard LMS reports can tell you

  • Whether a student visited a page or module item, and roughly when.
  • Submission status and grades for assignments and quizzes.
  • Overall activity trends for a student or a course.

Video hosted on a platform such as Kaltura, Panopto or Canvas Studio usually adds viewing data from that platform: plays, minutes watched and sometimes per-viewer completion.

What they usually miss

  • Understanding. Minutes watched is not comprehension. A student can play a video to the end with the tab in the background.
  • Where students get stuck. Which moments are re-watched, commented on or followed by wrong answers.
  • Interaction. Questions students asked and answered at specific moments, and instructor responses.
  • One view across platforms. Courses that mix YouTube, Vimeo and institutional video end up with data in several places, or none.

Four levels of video engagement data

LevelQuestion it answersExample measure
1. AccessDid they open it?Page views
2. ViewingDid they watch it?Completion percentage, segments watched
3. InteractionDid they engage with it?Questions answered, comments, notes
4. UnderstandingDid they learn from it?Question accuracy per concept, quality of reflections

Most courses stop at level 1 or 2. The most useful decisions, such as which explanation to re-record and which student to contact, need levels 3 and 4. Our learning analytics maturity model for video covers this in more depth.

  4. Read the analytics per video and per student. See which questions most students miss, where attention drops, and who has stopped participating. See engagement analytics.

What to look at each week

  • The two lowest-scoring questions. Usually a sign that the explanation before them needs work.
  • Students with no activity this week. A short message in week two is far more effective than one in week eight. See spotting at-risk students before week three.
  • The busiest timestamps. Clusters of comments or re-watches show where students need help, and where a clarification or an extra example would pay off.

Engagement data is only useful if it changes what happens next. Keep the weekly review to ten minutes, act on one thing, and let the next week's data show whether it worked.
